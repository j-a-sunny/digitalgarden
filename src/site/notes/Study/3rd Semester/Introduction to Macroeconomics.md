## Definition of Macroeconomics

- "Macro" comes from the Greek word *Macros*, meaning **larger**.
- Macroeconomics looks at the **economy as a whole**.
- It examines the factors that determine **national output** and its **growth over time**.

### Definitions by economists

| Economist | Definition (gist) |
| --- | --- |
| **R. G. D. Allen** | The study of relations between **broad economic aggregates** such as total employment, income and production. |
| **Edward Shapiro** | Its major task is explaining what determines the economy's **aggregate output of goods and services**; it deals with the functioning of the economy as a whole. |
| **Augbum** | A branch of economics which deals with the **economy as a whole**. |
| **K. E. Boulding** | Studies the **overall averages and aggregates** of the economic system. Not individual incomes but **national income**; not individual prices but the **price level**; not individual output but **national output**. |

> [!tip] Quick memory aid
> Micro = individual income / price / output.
> Macro = national income / price level / national output.

## The Birth of Macroeconomics

- Every discussion of macroeconomic policy begins with **John Maynard Keynes (1883–1946)**.
- Before Keynes, most economists and policy makers accepted the **highs and lows of business cycles as inevitable tides**.
- Macroeconomics emerged as a distinct branch during the **Great Depression of the 1930s**, when traditional theories failed to explain widespread unemployment and economic collapse.
- Keynes's key work: ***The General Theory of Employment, Interest and Money*** (**1936**).

### Keynes's twofold argument

1. High unemployment and **underutilized capacity can persist** in market economies.
2. Government **fiscal and monetary policies can affect output**, thereby reducing unemployment and shortening economic downturns.

## Objectives, Variables and Instruments

Two guiding questions:
- How do economists **evaluate** an economy's overall performance?
- What **tools** can governments use to pursue their economic goals?

### Objectives vs Instruments

| Objectives | Instruments |
| --- | --- |
| **Output**: high level and rapid growth | **Monetary policy**: control of money supply; affecting interest rates |
| **Employment**: high employment, low unemployment | **Fiscal policy**: government expenditure; taxation |
| **Price-level stability**: stable prices over time | |

### Macro variables

Macroeconomic variables are the variables that shape macroeconomics. Major ones:

- Aggregate output / production
- Aggregate demand
- Aggregate supply
- National income
- Aggregate savings
- Aggregate consumption
- Aggregate price
- Aggregate investment
- Aggregate expenditure
- Inflation

### Tools of macroeconomic policy

> [!note] Definition
> A **policy instrument** is an economic variable under the control of government that can affect one or more macroeconomic goals.

Governments use these tools to reduce inflation and unemployment, speed growth, or correct trade imbalances.

#### Monetary policy
- The **major instrument** of macroeconomic policy.
- Conducted through management of the nation's **money, credit and banking system**.
- By changing the money supply, the **central bank** influences stock prices, interest rates, housing prices and foreign exchange rates.

#### Fiscal policy
- The use of **taxes** and **government expenditures**.
- Government expenditure has two forms:
  1. **Government purchases** of goods and services.
  2. **Transfer payments**, which boost the incomes of targeted groups (e.g. the elderly, the unemployed).
- **Taxation** affects people's **disposable (spendable) incomes**.

## Some Macroeconomic Concepts

### Aggregate Demand (AD)

- The **total demand for goods and services** within a period (usually a year).
- Often viewed as **aggregate expenditure**: the total money needed to buy all goods and services in a country in that period.

> [!important] Formula (in words from the slides)
> **AD = Consumption + Investment + Government expenditure + Net exports**
> i.e. **AD = C + I + G + NX**

#### Components of AD

| Component | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| **1. Consumption** | Most important component. Determined by **disposable income** (= personal income − personal tax). |
| **2. Investment** | Spending on machinery, factories, software and inventory. Indicators: production level, expenditure on capital, future expectations. |
| **3. Government expenditure** | Government purchases of goods and services (e.g. judges' services, arms, tanks, equipment). |
| **4. Net exports** | **Export income − import expenditure**. |

#### Determinants of AD

| # | Determinant | Effect on AD |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | **Rate of interest** | Interest ↓ → AD ↑ (and vice versa) |
| 2 | **Expectation of profit** | Expected profit ↑ → AD ↑ |
| 3 | **Foreign exchange rate** | Exchange rate of domestic currency ↑ → AD ↑ (as stated on the slides) |
| 4 | **Population** | Population ↑ → AD ↑ |
| 5 | **Aggregate wealth** | Wealth ↑ → AD ↑ |
| 6 | **Expected inflation** | Expected inflation ↑ → AD ↑ |
| 7 | **Quantity of money** | Money ↑ → AD ↑ |
| 8 | **Tax** | Tax ↑ → AD ↓ (and vice versa) |
| 9 | **Income of other countries** | Foreign income ↑ → they buy more from us → AD ↑ |
| 10 | **Government expenditure** | Gov. spending ↑ → AD ↑ |
| 11 | **Transfer payments** | Transfers ↑ → AD ↑ |
| 12 | **Price level** | Price level ↑ → AD ↓ (and vice versa) |

> [!tip] Pattern
> Only **interest rate, tax and price level** move in the *opposite* direction to AD. Everything else moves the *same* direction.

### Aggregate Supply (AS)

- The **total goods and services all producers of a country are ready to produce** at a certain price level in a certain period.
- **Directly related to price level**: price ↑ → producers produce more → AS ↑; price ↓ → AS ↓.

#### Determinants of AS

| # | Determinant | Higher / better → | Lower / worse → |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | **Technology** | Improved technology → AS higher | Backward technology → AS lower |
| 2 | **Labor** | Larger labor force → AS higher | Smaller labor force → AS lower |
| 3 | **Infrastructure** | Developed → AS higher | Weak → AS lower |
| 4 | **Weather** | Favorable → AS higher | Unfavorable → AS lower |
| 5 | **Capital** | Higher capital stock → AS higher | Lower capital → AS lower |
| 6 | **Availability of raw materials** | Available → AS higher | Scarce → AS lower |
| 7 | **Education system** | Quality education → better human resources → AS higher | Poor quality → AS lower |
| 8 | **Law and order** | Peaceful → AS higher | Poor → AS lower |
| 9 | **Political stability** | Stable → AS higher | Unstable → AS lower |

## The AD-AS model

> [!note] Extra, not in the slides
> The slides explain aggregate demand and aggregate supply separately. This section shows how they work together.

The economy settles where **AD = AS**. That point gives the **equilibrium price level (P\*)** and **equilibrium real output (Y\*)**.

```
 P ^
   |
   |  AD \       /  AS
   |      \     /
   |       \   /
   |        \ /
 P*|- - - - -X- - - - - -   X = equilibrium
   |        / \
   |       /   \
   |      /     \
   +------------------------> Y (real output)
              Y*
```

- **AD slopes downward**: price level ↑ → AD ↓ (determinant 12 in the table above).
- **AS slopes upward**: price level ↑ → producers produce more.

### What shifts the curves?

| Event | Curve that shifts | Price level | Real output |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Interest rate ↓, tax ↓, government spending ↑, money supply ↑, foreign income ↑ | **AD shifts right** | ↑ | ↑ |
| The opposite of the above | **AD shifts left** | ↓ | ↓ |
| Better technology, more labor or capital, better infrastructure, favorable weather, stable politics | **AS shifts right** | ↓ | ↑ |
| Raw material shortage, unfavorable weather, political instability, poor law and order | **AS shifts left** | ↑ | ↓ |

> [!tip] Exam tip
> A change in the **price level** causes a **movement along** a curve. A change in any **other determinant** causes the **whole curve to shift**.

## The business cycle

> [!note] Extra, not in the slides
> The slides say fluctuations are a feature of capitalist economies and that Keynes rejected the idea that they are inevitable. This section names the phases.

| Phase | Output | Employment | Typical policy response |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Expansion** | Rising | Unemployment falling | Watch for rising prices |
| **Peak** | Highest point, near full capacity | Very high employment | **Contractionary**: raise interest rates or taxes to cool inflation |
| **Contraction / recession** | Falling | Unemployment rising | **Expansionary**: cut interest rates, raise government spending, cut taxes |
| **Trough** | Lowest point | Highest unemployment | Expansionary policy continues until recovery |
| **Recovery** | Starts rising again | Unemployment starts falling | Gradually withdraw support |

- A **depression** is a very deep, prolonged contraction, like the **Great Depression of the 1930s**.
- A common textbook rule of thumb: a **recession** is two or more consecutive quarters of falling real GDP.
- Keynes's argument: fiscal and monetary policy can **shorten downturns**, so cycles are not simply inevitable tides.

## Micro vs macro

> [!note] Extra, not in the slides

| Basis | Microeconomics | Macroeconomics |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Unit of study** | Individual consumers, households, firms and single markets | The economy as a whole (aggregates) |
| **Central question** | How are the price and output of a particular good determined? | What determines national output, employment and the price level? |
| **Key variables** | Price of a good, individual income, a firm's output, demand and supply of one product | National income, aggregate demand and supply, general price level, inflation, unemployment |
| **Main concern** | Resource allocation and efficiency | Growth, full employment and price stability |
| **Policy tools** | Specific taxes, subsidies, price controls | Monetary policy and fiscal policy |
| **Origin** | Older classical and neoclassical tradition | Distinct branch from the 1930s (Keynes) |
| **Example question** | Why did the price of rice rise? | Why did overall inflation rise? |

> [!important] Fallacy of composition (limitation 2)
> **Paradox of thrift**: one household that saves more becomes better off. If **everyone** saves more, spending falls, aggregate demand falls, incomes fall, and total saving may not rise. What is true for the part is false for the whole.

The two are linked, as the slides say: macro results rest on the behavior of many individual units, and micro analysis needs the aggregate setting.

## Importance / Issues / Scope

1. **Functioning of an economy**: helps explain how a complex modern economy works, and how **national income and employment** are determined by aggregate demand and aggregate supply.
2. **Formulation of economic policies**:
   - The days of *laissez-faire* are over; government intervention is an accomplished fact.
   - Governments deal with **groups and masses**, not individuals.
   - Example: in a **depression** (idle machines, jobless people), macroeconomics helps analyze the causes and choose suitable policies.
3. **Understanding microeconomics**:
   - No macroeconomic law can be framed without first studying aggregates.
   - A theory of the firm needs the behavior of **several firms**, not one.
   - Analogy: a **forest** is an aggregation of trees but doesn't show the behavior of individual trees.
   - Microeconomics remains "a jungle of special assumptions, special cases, unsatisfactory measurements and abstract theorizing".
4. **Understanding and controlling economic fluctuations**:
   - Fluctuations are characteristic of **capitalist** societies.
   - Studying them requires aggregate consumption, saving and investment.
   - Helps analyze causes of fluctuations in income, output and employment and **reduce their severity**.
5. **Inflation and deflation**: different sections of society are affected differently by changes in the value of money; macro analysis helps counteract the adverse effects.
6. **Study of national income**:
   - Brought out the importance of **national income and social accounts**.
   - Key indicator of a country's whole economy.
   - Revealed that **three-fourths of the world lives in abject poverty** (per the slides).
   - Correct economic policies are not possible without it.
7. **Study of economic development**: draws attention to **underdeveloped countries**, and reveals inequalities of wealth within economies and differences in living standards between countries.
8. **Performance of an economy**:
   - Result-oriented study of actual achievements.
   - **GNP / National Income** estimates compare production across periods.
   - The composition of GNP shows each sector's contribution.
9. **Nature of material welfare**:
   - Studies the nature and size of nations' material welfare.
   - Measuring social welfare is hard; even welfare economics doesn't fully help.
   - When the aim of economics is the welfare of the whole society, economics becomes macroeconomics.
10. **Balance of payments**: explains its determinants, identifies the causes of deficits and suggests remedies.
11. **Solving economic problems**: poverty, unemployment, business cycles, etc., can be solved only at the **level of the whole economy**.

---

## Limitations / Exceptions

1. **Ignores individual welfare.** Example: raising national saving at the cost of individual welfare is not considered a wise policy.
2. **Fallacy of composition**: propositions true for individuals or small groups often turn out **false for the whole economy**.
3. **Treats aggregates as homogeneous** and ignores their internal composition. Example: if clerks' wages fall and teachers' wages rise, the **average wage can stay the same**.
4. **Not all aggregate variables are equally important.** Example: national income can rise just because a few rich families earned more, without individual incomes rising generally.
5. **Models mostly reflect developed countries.** Developing countries face different realities and benefit less from them.

## Bangladesh examples

> [!warning] Illustrative only
> These link the theory to the Bangladesh economy in general terms. I left out numbers on purpose. Use current data from Bangladesh Bank, the Bangladesh Bureau of Statistics or your course material before quoting figures in an exam or assignment.

| Concept | Bangladesh illustration |
| --- | --- |
| **Monetary policy** | **Bangladesh Bank**, the central bank, manages money, credit and banking. It uses its policy rate and other tools to influence interest rates and credit, and announces its stance in its Monetary Policy Statement. |
| **Fiscal policy** | The **national budget** is presented each year for the fiscal year starting 1 July. It sets taxes (income tax, VAT, customs duties) and government spending. |
| **Transfer payments** | Social safety net programs such as old-age and widow allowances and student stipends raise the incomes of targeted groups. |
| **Consumption** | **Remittances** from workers abroad raise household disposable income, which supports consumption and aggregate demand. |
| **Net exports** | **Ready-made garments** dominate exports, while fuel, capital machinery and some food items are major imports. |
| **Exchange rate** | If the taka **depreciates**, exports become cheaper abroad and imports dearer, so net exports tend to rise (AD ↑). But costlier imported inputs raise production costs (AS ↓). |
| **Population** | A large population supports aggregate demand. |
| **AS: infrastructure** | Power and gas supply, ports and roads strongly affect how much firms can produce. |
| **AS: weather** | Floods and cyclones can damage agriculture and cut supply. |
| **AS: law and order and political stability** | Strikes, blockades and unrest disrupt production and transport. |
| **AS: labor and education** | A large young workforce raises potential supply, but skills and education quality decide how much it adds. |
| **Inflation** | Rising food and fuel prices hit households unevenly, which is the slides' point about inflation affecting different sections of society differently. |
| **Limitation 4** | Higher national income or GDP does not show whether the gains are spread evenly. Income distribution can hide behind the average. |
| **Limitation 5** | A large informal sector and the weight of agriculture and remittances mean models built for developed economies fit only partly. |

## Glossary

> [!note] Extra, not in the slides

| Term | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| **Aggregate** | A total for the whole economy, such as total output or total consumption |
| **GDP** (Gross Domestic Product) | Value of final goods and services produced **within a country** in a period |
| **GNP** (Gross National Product) | GDP **plus net income earned from abroad**; measures output by a country's **residents** |
| **National income** | Total income earned by a country's residents in a period |
| **Real vs nominal** | Nominal uses current prices; real is adjusted for inflation |
| **Disposable income** | Personal income minus personal tax |
| **Transfer payment** | Government payment with no good or service in return, such as an allowance or pension |
| **Net exports** | Export income minus import expenditure |
| **Investment** | Spending on machinery, factories, software and inventory (not buying shares) |
| **Laissez-faire** | The view that government should not intervene in the economy |
| **Inflation** | A sustained rise in the general price level |
| **Deflation** | A sustained fall in the general price level |
| **Stagflation** | High inflation together with high unemployment and weak growth |
| **Business cycle** | Recurring ups and downs in output and employment |
| **Recession** | A significant fall in output; often defined as two consecutive quarters of falling real GDP |
| **Depression** | A very severe and prolonged recession |
| **Monetary policy** | Control of money supply and interest rates by the central bank |
| **Fiscal policy** | Use of government spending and taxation |
| **Central bank** | The institution that manages the money supply and banking system (Bangladesh Bank in Bangladesh) |
| **Balance of payments** | Record of all a country's economic transactions with the rest of the world |
| **Full employment** | Everyone who wants a job at the going wage can find one |
| **Policy instrument** | A variable the government controls that can affect macro goals |

## Exam questions with model answers

> [!note] Extra, not in the slides
> Answers are built from the lecture content. Expand each point with an example in the exam.

> [!example]- Q1. Define macroeconomics and distinguish it from microeconomics.
> **Definition:** "Macro" comes from the Greek *Macros* (larger). It studies the economy as a whole: national output, income, employment and the price level. Allen calls it the study of relations between broad aggregates; Boulding says it deals with national income, not individual incomes, the price level, not individual prices, and national output, not individual output.
> **Difference:** micro studies individual units such as a consumer, a firm or one market. Macro studies totals such as GDP, aggregate demand and inflation. (See the [[Study/3rd Semester/Introduction to Macroeconomics#Micro vs macro\|#Micro vs macro]] table.)

> [!example]- Q2. Discuss the importance of macroeconomics.
> 1. Explains how the whole economy functions and how income and employment are determined.
> 2. Guides economic policy now that laissez-faire is over.
> 3. Helps understand microeconomics, since aggregates come first.
> 4. Helps understand and control economic fluctuations.
> 5. Helps analyze inflation and deflation.
> 6. National income study shows the state of the economy and the extent of poverty.
> 7. Draws attention to development and inequality between countries.
> 8. Measures the performance of the economy (GNP, national income).
> 9. Helps study material welfare.
> 10. Explains balance of payments problems and remedies.
> 11. Helps solve problems such as poverty, unemployment and business cycles that only make sense at the economy level.

> [!example]- Q3. Explain the birth of macroeconomics and Keynes's contribution.
> Before Keynes, economists accepted business cycles as inevitable. The **Great Depression of the 1930s** exposed the failure of traditional theory to explain mass unemployment. In **The General Theory of Employment, Interest and Money (1936)**, Keynes argued (1) high unemployment and idle capacity can persist in market economies, and (2) government fiscal and monetary policy can raise output, reduce unemployment and shorten downturns.

> [!example]- Q4. What are the objectives and instruments of macroeconomic policy?
> **Objectives:** high and rapid output growth; high employment and low unemployment; stable price level.
> **Instruments:** monetary policy (control of money supply, influence on interest rates, through the central bank) and fiscal policy (government expenditure, which includes purchases and transfer payments, and taxation, which changes disposable income).

> [!example]- Q5. What is aggregate demand? Explain its components and determinants.
> **Aggregate demand** is the total desired expenditure on goods and services in an economy in a year: **AD = C + I + G + NX**.
> **Components:** consumption (driven by disposable income), investment, government purchases, net exports.
> **Determinants:** interest rate, expected profit, exchange rate, population, wealth, expected inflation, money supply, taxes, foreign income, government spending, transfer payments, price level. Interest rate, tax and price level have an inverse relationship with AD; the others are direct. (See the [[Study/3rd Semester/Introduction to Macroeconomics#Some Macroeconomic Concepts\|#Some Macroeconomic Concepts]] table.)

> [!example]- Q6. What is aggregate supply? Explain its determinants.
> **Aggregate supply** is the total goods and services producers are willing to produce at a given price level in a period. It rises with the price level.
> **Determinants:** technology, labor, infrastructure, weather, capital, raw materials, education, law and order, political stability. Better conditions raise AS; worse conditions lower it.

> [!example]- Q7. Explain the limitations of macroeconomics.
> 1. Ignores individual welfare.
> 2. What is true for individuals may be false for the whole economy (fallacy of composition, e.g. paradox of thrift).
> 3. Treats aggregates as homogeneous and hides their internal composition (clerks' wages fall, teachers' rise, average unchanged).
> 4. Not all aggregates are equally important; rising national income may reflect gains for a few rich families.
> 5. Models are built for developed countries and fit developing countries less well.

> [!example]- Q8. How do fiscal and monetary policy differ?
> **Monetary policy** is run by the central bank through the money supply, credit and interest rates. **Fiscal policy** is run by the government through taxes and spending (purchases and transfers). Monetary policy mainly works through financial variables; fiscal policy works directly through incomes and spending.
