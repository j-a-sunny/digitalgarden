> [!summary] Summary
> **Nonparametric (distribution-free) tests** do not assume a particular population distribution such as the normal. They work with signs, ranks or counts, so they suit nominal or ordinal data and small samples. The slides cover two tests: the **sign test** (one sample, tests a median) and the **median test** (two independent groups, uses a $2\times2$ $\chi^2$).

## Parametric vs nonparametric

| Basis | Parametric | Nonparametric |
|---|---|---|
| Test statistic based on | the distribution of the data | arbitrary (no fixed distribution) |
| Measurement level | interval or ratio | nominal or ordinal |
| Measure of centre | mean | median |
| Information about population | completely known | unavailable |
| Applies to | variables | variables and attributes |
| Correlation | Pearson's coefficient | Spearman's rank correlation |

**Rule of thumb from the slides:** if the population is fully described by parameters, use a parametric test. If you know nothing about it, use a nonparametric test.

| Advantages | Disadvantages |
|---|---|
| No need to assume a normal (or other) shape | If all assumptions of a parametric test hold, a nonparametric test wastes information |
| Works with small samples | No nonparametric test for interaction effects in ANOVA |
| Few assumptions, so little risk of misuse | Calculations are simple but can be long and tedious |
| Quick and easy, little mathematics needed | |
| Fine for weak measurement scales | |

## The sign test (one sample)

Tests a hypothesis about the **median** $M$ using only the **signs** of $X_i-M_0$.

**Assumptions:** random sample; ordinal scale; values treated as continuous.

| Case | $H_0$ | $H_1$ | Test statistic $k$ | Reject $H_0$ if |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| (a) two-sided | $M=M_0$ | $M\ne M_0$ | smaller of the number of + and − signs | $k\le$ critical value |
| (b) one-sided | $M=M_0$ | $M>M_0$ | number of **−** signs | $k\le$ critical value |
| (c) one-sided | $M=M_0$ | $M<M_0$ | number of **+** signs | $k\le$ critical value |

- Values equal to $M_0$ are **dropped** (and $n$ is reduced).
- Under $H_0$ we expect about as many + as − signs. Too few of one sign is evidence against $H_0$.
- Equivalent rule: reject if the probability of seeing that few of the rarer sign is $\le\alpha$. The critical value comes from the sign-test table.

> [!warning] Note the direction
> Unlike $t$ and $F$ tests, here you reject when the statistic is **small**: $k\le$ C.V.

### Example 1: student work hours (one-sided)
15 students' weekly work hours: 18, 16, 15, 13, 9, 15, 13, 11, 15, 18, 10, 14, 20, 14, 12. Claim: median > 12. $\alpha=0.05$.

- $H_0:M=12$, $H_1:M>12$ (claim).
- Signs against 12: **+** for 11 values (13 to 20), **−** for 3 values (9, 10, 11), one value equal to 12 is dropped. $n=11+3=14$.
- Test statistic = number of − signs: $k=3$. Critical value (n=14, one-tailed 5%) $=3$.
- $k\le$ C.V. so **reject $H_0$**. There is evidence the median weekly work hours exceed 12.

### Example 2: study hours before a test (two-sided)
12 responses: 6, 5, 1, 2, 2, 5, 7, 5, 3, 7, 4, 7. $H_0:M=3$, $H_1:M\ne3$. $\alpha=0.05$.

- Signs against 3: + for 8 values, − for 3 values (1, 2, 2), one value equals 3 (dropped). $n=11$.
- $k=\min(8,3)=3$. Critical value for $n=11$, two-tailed 5% $=1$.
- $k=3>1$, so **do not reject $H_0$**.

> [!warning] Slide error
> The slide's comment says "there is evidence to support the claim that the median is 3". The claim here is $H_1:M\ne3$. Because we fail to reject $H_0$, the correct conclusion is: **there is not enough evidence that the median differs from 3**, i.e. the data are consistent with a median of 3 hours.

## The median test (two independent groups)

Tests whether two independent samples come from populations with the **same median**. Needs at least ordinal data.
$H_0:M_1=M_2$, $H_1:M_1\ne M_2$.

**Procedure:**
1. Pool both samples and find the combined median.
2. Count, for each group, scores above the median and scores at or below it.
3. Put the counts in a $2\times2$ table.

| | Group I | Group II | Total |
|---|---|---|---|
| Above median | $a$ | $b$ | $a+b$ |
| At or below median | $c$ | $d$ | $c+d$ |
| Total | $a+c=n_1$ | $b+d=n_2$ | $n$ |

$$\chi^2=\frac{n(ad-bc)^2}{(a+b)(a+c)(b+d)(c+d)},\ \ 1\text{ d.f.}$$
If $n<40$ use the continuity correction:
$$\chi_c^2=\frac{n\left(\lvert ad-bc\rvert-\tfrac n2\right)^2}{(a+b)(a+c)(b+d)(c+d)}$$
A significant $\chi^2$ means reject $H_0$.

### Example 3: arithmetic scores of two groups
X: 12, 16, 18, 7, 6, 4, 11, 12, 8, 20, 18, 16, 10 (13 learners). Y: 7, 12, 14, 18, 5, 16, 9, 10, 14, 3, 18, 9, 7, 4 (14 learners). Combined median $=11$.

| | X | Y | Total |
|---|---|---|---|
| Above 11 | 7 | 6 | 13 |
| At or below 11 | 6 | 8 | 14 |
| Total | 13 | 14 | 27 |

$$\chi_c^2=\frac{27\left(\lvert 56-36\rvert-13.5\right)^2}{13\cdot14\cdot13\cdot14}=0.034$$
Not significant (table value 3.84 for 1 d.f.), so **do not reject $H_0$**: no evidence the medians differ.

> [!tip] Added: memory aids
> - **Sign test: count the signs, throw away the ties, reject when one sign is rare.**
> - **Median test = $\chi^2$ on a 2×2 table split at the pooled median.** Same formula as the $2\times2$ shortcut in [[Study/3rd Semester/Applied Business Statistics/1739799506338_Test of hypothesis\|1739799506338_Test of hypothesis]].
> - For $n<40$, always use the correction.

> [!question]- Self-test 1: Why drop observations equal to $M_0$?
> They carry no sign (neither + nor −), so they give no information about which side of $M_0$ the median is on.

> [!question]- Self-test 2: In a sign test for $H_1:M<M_0$, which sign count is the test statistic?
> The number of **plus** signs. Very few plus signs would mean most values are below $M_0$.

> [!question]- Self-test 3: When is a nonparametric test preferred to a parametric one?
> When the population shape is unknown or clearly non-normal, the data are ordinal or nominal, or the sample is small.

> [!question]- Self-test 4: In the median test, how many d.f. does $\chi^2$ have?
> One, since the table is $2\times2$.
